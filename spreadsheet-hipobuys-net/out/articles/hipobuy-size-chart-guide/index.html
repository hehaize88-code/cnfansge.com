Hipobuy Size Chart Guide: Clothing and Shoe Measurements

There is no single Hipobuy size chart that fits every seller. Compare the exact listing with a garment or shoe that already fits, then confirm the same measurements in warehouse photos before international shipping.

Start with the chart for the exact product

A Hipobuy size chart search often begins with a simple question: which size should I buy? The useful answer starts with the seller and the selected product. A shopping agent can help purchase items from different marketplace sellers, but that does not make their measurements consistent. Two products marked medium can have different chest widths, lengths, cuts and measurement methods. A familiar label is a starting point, not enough evidence to choose.

Open the current listing and find the chart attached to the exact item or version. Save the image and note whether its numbers describe a body, a finished garment, a foot or a shoe interior. Check the unit and the measurement points. If a chart is missing, blurred or shared across several products without explanation, ask for clarification before paying. A later warehouse measurement is useful, but it does not make an unclear initial purchase risk-free.

Build a reference from something that fits

For clothing, measure an item you already wear comfortably. Lay it flat on a level surface, smooth ordinary wrinkles without stretching the fabric, and use the same starting points shown in the seller diagram. Record chest width, body length and any other dimension that makes the fit work. For trousers, that might include the waist measured relaxed, inseam and rise. For an oversized hoodie, shoulder width and sleeve construction also matter.

Write down the reference item, the method and the unit together. A number without a method is easy to misuse later. Photograph the tape if you want a repeatable record. Measure again if the fabric was pulled tight or the tape began inside the seam. Keep a separate reference for garments with different cuts. A fitted shirt should not be the only benchmark for an oversized hoodie, and an elastic waistband should be recorded both with its measurement method and the condition specified by the seller. Update the record if your reference garment changes after washing. Your aim is not laboratory precision; it is a consistent comparison between the item you like, the seller chart and the warehouse evidence.

Separate flat width from body circumference

A garment laid flat may be measured from one armpit to the other. A body chart may instead list the full chest circumference. Those numbers describe different things. As a simple geometry example, a flat chest width of 56 cm corresponds to roughly 112 cm around that part of the garment when doubled. It does not mean the garment is designed for a person with a 112 cm chest, because the cut and intended room still matter.

Do not compare half-width directly with a full circumference and conclude that the seller made an error. First identify what each column measures. Keep the seller’s wording alongside your notes if the translation is unclear. When you need to convert inches to centimetres, multiply by 2.54; divide centimetres by 2.54 for inches. Unit conversion fixes units only. It does not convert one brand’s fit into another brand’s fit.

Choose room for the way you wear the garment

Fit depends on use as well as dimensions. A close-fitting base layer and a hoodie worn over a shirt do not need the same room. Decide whether the new item should match your reference, feel slightly looser or allow another layer. Compare the dimensions responsible for that difference instead of automatically choosing one label larger. Increasing the label can add unwanted body or sleeve length without solving a tight shoulder.

Use a small acceptable range for each important measurement and note which limit would make the item unusable. Those limits are personal planning choices, not universal tolerances. For example, you might accept a slightly longer body but reject sleeves that cover your hands. Check any seller-stated measurement allowance separately. Do not use an arbitrary allowance to excuse a mismatch that defeats the reason you chose the item.

Read shoe measurements before converting labels

For shoes, start with the sizing method supplied for that exact model. Measure both feet using a consistent method and compare the longer measurement with the seller’s stated foot-length guidance when available. Keep socks and intended use in mind when following the seller’s fitting instructions. EU, UK and US labels can differ by brand and product category; a generic conversion chart should not override a model-specific chart.

Foot length, removable insole length and outsole length are different measurements. An outsole includes material beyond the usable interior. An insole can curve or extend into space that does not provide the same usable fit. Ask which measurement is shown before comparing numbers. If warehouse staff can measure an insole, request a clear view from heel to toe with the tape aligned. Treat it as additional evidence, not a guaranteed comfort test.

Send a precise warehouse measurement request

A useful request names the item, the dimension and the endpoints. For a sweatshirt, ask for flat chest width from armpit to armpit and body length from the agreed shoulder point to the hem. For trousers, ask for the same waist and inseam method used on your reference. State that the fabric should lie flat without being stretched. Check whether the account currently offers the request and what it costs before submitting it.

When the photo arrives, look at the zero point, the endpoint and the entire tape. A close crop can hide where the measurement started. A diagonal tape can exaggerate a width, while a fold can shorten it. If the result sits close to your limit, ask a targeted follow-up that resolves the uncertainty. Several unrelated photos do less for a fit decision than one clear measurement using the right method.

  • Save the seller chart and exact selected size.
  • Record the same measurements on a reference item.
  • Name measurement points in the warehouse request.
  • Check the tape placement before interpreting the number.

Work through a clothing example

Imagine a sweatshirt you like measures 58 cm across the chest and 69 cm in body length. The new listing offers one size at 56 and 68 cm and another at 59 and 71 cm. This is an illustration, not a current product chart. If you want similar room but can accept a little more length, the second option deserves closer attention. If 71 cm is too long for your preferred shape, neither option may be the right purchase.

The decision still needs the shoulder and sleeve information when those areas affect fit. After ordering, compare the received item with the saved chart and your acceptable range. A warehouse result that differs from the listing should trigger a check of the measurement method before a conclusion. If the mismatch is real, ask about the seller’s available return or exchange process while the item is still in the warehouse.

Make the fit decision before shipping

Keep a short record with four columns: reference measurement, seller measurement, warehouse measurement and your decision. That makes it easier to spot when you compared different methods or relied on a label instead of a number. Keep uncertain measurements marked as uncertain. An attractive product photo should not erase a missing dimension that matters to whether you can use the item.

Once the fit evidence is adequate, move to the parcel decision. Include domestic delivery, any measurement service and international freight when deciding whether the purchase still makes sense. If the size cannot be established or the received dimensions are unsuitable, resolve the issue before paying to send it farther. A useful sizing process ends with a deliberate choice: accept, request clearer evidence, exchange where available, or leave the item out of the parcel.
